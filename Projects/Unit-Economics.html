01 · THE QUESTION

Every growth plan is a bet on one customer, multiplied

A company's P&L is the sum of thousands of small transactions. Each customer costs something to win, pays something over a lifetime, and costs something to serve. Unit economics is the discipline of looking at one of those customers in isolation and asking a plain question: do we make money on this person, and how long does it take?

The answer matters more as a company grows, because growth multiplies whatever the unit already is. If each customer returns $3 of gross profit for every $1 of acquisition spend, scaling up creates value. If each customer returns $0.80, scaling up destroys it faster. Growth does not fix a broken unit. It turns a small, survivable loss into a large one.

There is a second, less obvious point, and it is the reason this paper exists. Two businesses can have identical lifetime economics and still need very different amounts of money to grow. The difference is time: how many months pass between paying to acquire a customer and earning that money back. While a company is growing, it is always paying for next month's customers before this month's have paid for themselves. That gap is the cash a founder has to raise.

LTV:CAC tells you whether a customer is worth acquiring. Payback tells you how much capital it takes to acquire them at scale. You need both.

The rest of the paper builds this up in order. Section 02 defines the seven numbers every unit economics case rests on. Section 03 works through a SaaS example where three product lines look identical on LTV:CAC and very different on cash. Section 05 turns to economies of scale, and section 06 applies the whole method to a direct-to-consumer brand with a full 36-month P&L. Sections 07 to 11 are a reusable playbook for building the case for any company.

02 · DEFINITIONS

The seven numbers a unit economics case rests on

Most disagreements about unit economics are really disagreements about definitions. Before building anything, write down exactly how each number is calculated, what is included, and over what period. The formulas below are the ones used in the companion model.

CAC cost to acquire a customer

Everything spent to win new customers in a period, divided by the new customers won in that period. "Everything" means paid media, sales salaries and commissions, agency fees, tools, and the marketing team.

CAC = Sales & marketing spend ÷ New customers

Trap: quoting only paid-media CAC, or dividing by all new customers when many came organically. Report both blended and paid CAC.

ARPU / AOV revenue per unit

What a customer pays per period (SaaS) or per order (commerce). For a marketplace the unit is a transaction and the revenue is the take rate on its value.

ARPU = Revenue ÷ Active customers

Trap: using list price instead of realised price after discounts, refunds and free months.

Gross / contribution margin what you keep

The share of each payment left after the costs that rise with every unit sold: product cost, hosting, payment fees, shipping, support. Contribution margin goes further and also subtracts fulfilment and other variable costs.

CM = Net revenue − All variable costs

Trap: computing LTV on revenue. A customer worth $1,000 of revenue at 30% margin is worth $300 to you.

Churn & retention how long they stay

Monthly churn is the share of customers active at the start of a month who are gone by the end. Retention is its complement, and for commerce it is better measured as repeat-order rate by cohort.

Lifetime (months) = 1 ÷ Monthly churn

Trap: averaging churn across all customers. New customers churn faster than old ones, so blended churn flatters a fast-growing base.

LTV lifetime value

The total margin a customer generates over their life with you. In the simple steady-state form it is monthly margin times expected lifetime.

LTV = ARPU × Gross margin % ÷ Monthly churn

Trap: an infinite or 10-year horizon on a two-year-old company. Cap LTV at a horizon you have data for (24 or 36 months).

LTV : CAC return on acquisition

Lifetime margin earned per dollar of acquisition spend. A widely used benchmark is 3x or higher. Below 1x, every new customer loses money.

LTV:CAC = LTV ÷ CAC

Trap: treating it as sufficient. A 3.2x line can still need $3.8M of funding, as section 03 shows.

CAC payback months to recover CAC

How many months of margin it takes to earn back what you spent acquiring the customer. Under 12 months is a common target for SaaS; consumer brands often aim for the first or second order.

Payback = CAC ÷ (ARPU × Gross margin %)

Trap: computing payback on revenue instead of margin, which makes it look two to five times shorter than it is.

03 · CASE A

Three SaaS lines with identical LTV:CAC

This example is adapted from David Skok's teaching model on For Entrepreneurs. A software company sells three product lines. Each costs $6,000 to acquire a customer and earns 80% gross margin. Working through the four calculations

Customer lifetime. If 5% of Blue's customers leave each month, the average customer stays 1 ÷ 0.05 = 20 months. Halve the churn and lifetime doubles, so Red's customers stay 40 months and Green's 60.

fx=1/C8

LTV. Each Blue customer pays $1,200 a month, of which the company keeps 80%, or $960. Over 20 months that is $19,200. Red keeps $480 a month for 40 months and Green $320 for 60. The arithmetic lands on $19,200 for all three because the price and lifetime offset each other exactly.

fx=C5*C6*C14

LTV : CAC. $19,200 ÷ $6,000 = 3.2x for every line. On the single most quoted metric in SaaS, the three are indistinguishable.

fx=C16/C7

Months to recover CAC. Blue earns back its $6,000 at $960 a month, so payback is 6.25 months. Red needs 12.5 months and Green 18.75. This is where the lines separate.

fx=C7/(C5*C6)

Why payback drives the cash low point

The model then builds 60 months of customers for each line. It starts with 10 new customers in month 1 and adds two more each month (10, 12, 14, and so on). Each month the company pays CAC on every new customer up front and collects gross profit from every active customer. Churned customers are removed at the line's churn rate, rounded to whole customers.

fxNet cash flow = Active × Payment × Margin − New × CAC

In the early months, acquisition spend is large and the installed base is small, so cash flow is negative. As the base grows, the gross profit it throws off eventually exceeds the cost of each month's new cohort. The cumulative cash curve falls, bottoms out, and turns up. The bottom of that curve is the most capital the business needs before it funds itself.

The longer the payback, the longer each customer sits "underwater" and the more cohorts are underwater at once. Blue's cash flow turns positive in month 12 and the curve bottoms at $405K. Red takes until month 28 and needs $1.65M. Green takes until month 43 and needs $3.78M, more than nine times Blue's requirement. At month 60 Blue has generated $21.3M of cumulative cash while Green is still $2.1M in the hole, even though Green's customers are, on paper, just as valuable.

The faster you grow, the deeper the hole. Growth makes the trough deeper and pushes it later, because each month's larger cohort must be funded before it pays back. This is why investors who see a long payback period ask how much growth the company can afford, and why a business with a 24-month payback can be "profitable per customer" and still run out of money.

Levers that shorten payback

  • Raise price or move up-market. Payback falls in direct proportion to monthly margin. It is the lever Blue has and Green lacks.
  • Bill annually up front. Collecting 12 months on day one can make cash payback immediate even when accounting payback is a year. This is the single most powerful cash lever in SaaS.
  • Lower CAC through channel mix. Referral, content and product-led acquisition usually cost a fraction of outbound sales.
  • Expansion revenue. Upsells and seat growth raise ARPU over a customer's life. Net revenue retention above 100% ("negative churn") means a cohort earns more each year than the last.
  • Improve gross margin. Cheaper hosting, self-serve onboarding and lower support load all raise the monthly margin that repays CAC.

05 · ECONOMIES OF SCALE

Scale changes the unit. Some costs fall, one usually rises.

A unit economics model with fixed inputs assumes the hundredth customer looks like the hundred-thousandth. In practice, scale moves almost every line of the unit, and not all in the same direction. A credible case shows which costs fall with volume, which rise, and where the net effect lands.

▼ fallsVariable cost per unit

Purchasing power. Larger orders earn supplier discounts. Density. More orders per delivery zone or warehouse cut fulfilment cost. Learning curve. Repeated processes get cheaper and lose less to waste and errors.

▼ fallsFixed cost per unit

Team, technology, rent and brand spend grow in steps while revenue grows continuously. The same platform serves ten times the customers. This is operating leverage, and it is why EBITDA margin can rise even when contribution margin is flat.

▲ risesCustomer acquisition cost

Channel saturation. The cheapest, highest-intent customers are acquired first. Each extra dollar of ad spend reaches a less interested audience at a higher price. Expanding to new geographies or segments resets awareness from zero.

Other forces can also push the unit up with scale: more complex operations, deeper discounting to sustain growth, lower-quality cohorts that churn faster, and competition for the same channels. On the favourable side, some businesses gain network effects (each user makes the product more valuable to the next) or data advantages that improve targeting and lower CAC over time. These are rarer than pitch decks suggest.

The useful way to model scale is to make each force an explicit, adjustable assumption. In the companion model, cost of goods and fulfilment fall in volume tiers, CAC rises with acquisition volume through an elasticity parameter, and fixed costs grow at 2% a month while revenue grows much faster. Section 06 shows what those three forces do to one business.

06 · CASE B

Hearth Coffee Co.: one order, one customer, one P&L

To show that the method transfers beyond SaaS, the second case applies it to a direct-to-consumer coffee brand. Hearth Coffee Co. is fictional, and its assumptions are illustrative values chosen to sit inside typical D2C ranges. It sells bags of coffee online at a $45 average order value, acquires customers through paid social and search, and relies on repeat orders because coffee is a replenishment product.

Commerce businesses build unit economics in three layers, each answering a different question:

  • CM1, gross profit: is the product itself profitable after discounts, returns and cost of goods?
  • CM2, contribution after variable costs: is each order profitable once it is picked, packed, shipped and paid for?
  • CM3, contribution after marketing: is the business profitable after paying to acquire customers, before fixed overhead?

The order: from $45 basket to $15 of contribution

At launch, only about a third of each order's net revenue survives as contribution. Shipping is the cost that hurts most: at $8.00 it is 18% of the basket and does not shrink when a customer buys a smaller bag. This is why D2C brands push bundles and free-shipping thresholds. AOV is the strongest lever against per-order fixed costs, and the AOV × COGS grid in the model's Sensitivity tab shows contribution rising from $8.95 at a $30 basket to $25.90 at $60 (both at 30% COGS).

The customer: payback in month six

A $38 CAC is far more than one order's $15.18 contribution, so the first order covers only 40% of acquisition cost. Hearth depends on repeat purchases. The model assumes 40% of a cohort reorders in its first month after acquisition, and that the reorder rate decays by 6% each month after that (a factor of 0.94). Over 24 months the average customer places 6.06 orders.

Read the P&L top to bottom and the three scale forces are all visible. CM2 margin climbs from 40% to 47% as the brand moves through cost tiers. Performance marketing stays the largest cost but falls as a share of revenue, because repeat orders from earlier cohorts need no new acquisition spend. Fixed costs fall from 60% of revenue in year 1 to 17% in year 3. Together these take EBITDA margin from −57% to +3.5%.

The cash trough comes one month before EBITDA turns positive, the same shape as the SaaS curves in section 03. The difference is that Hearth's trough is driven as much by fixed costs as by acquisition. A version of the business with lower overhead, or one that grew acquisition more slowly, would need far less capital. The model makes that trade-off explicit: every input in D2C_Inputs can be changed and the P&L, cohorts and trough recalculate.

07 · PLAYBOOK

How to build a unit economics case for any company

The two cases follow the same ten steps. The order matters: each step uses the output of the one before, and skipping ahead (to LTV, usually) is how most models go wrong.

  1. Define the unit

    Pick the smallest repeatable thing the business sells and acquires: a subscriber, an order, a ride, a loan, a seat. Everything else is measured per unit. A marketplace may need two units, one per side.

  2. Map the revenue driver

    Write revenue as a product of drivers you can observe: customers × ARPU for SaaS, orders × AOV for commerce, GMV × take rate for marketplaces, loan book × net interest margin for lenders. Use realised prices after discounts and refunds.

  3. List every variable cost and compute contribution margin

    Anything that rises with one more unit belongs here: product cost, hosting, payment fees, shipping, packaging, support time, rider pay, credit losses. Build the CM1 → CM2 waterfall so each layer is visible.

  4. Measure CAC honestly

    Use fully loaded sales and marketing cost, report blended and paid CAC separately, and show CAC by channel. Lag spend to the month it produces customers when the sales cycle is long.

  5. Build retention from cohorts

    Group customers by the month they joined and track what share is still active, or still ordering, each month after. A cohort table is the only reliable source of churn, and it shows whether newer cohorts behave better or worse than older ones.

  6. Compute LTV over a defensible horizon

    Multiply contribution per period by the retention curve and sum over 12, 24 or 36 months. Use the horizon your data supports. Apply a discount rate if the horizon is long or capital is expensive.

  7. Compute LTV:CAC and payback together

    LTV:CAC answers whether the unit creates value. Payback answers how long cash is tied up. A strong case reports both, plus the share of CAC recovered by the first purchase.

  8. Layer on fixed costs to get a P&L

    Add team, technology, rent and overhead below contribution. Show how fixed costs step up with scale. EBITDA and cumulative cash fall out of this layer, and the cash trough is the funding requirement.

  9. Model scale explicitly

    Make cost tiers, CAC elasticity and fixed-cost growth into adjustable assumptions. Show the unit at launch and at scale side by side so the reader can see which effects win.

  10. Stress-test the breakpoints

    Build sensitivity grids on the two or three inputs that matter most (usually churn, CAC and price or AOV). Find the values at which LTV:CAC falls below 1x or payback passes 24 months, and say how far today's numbers are from those lines.

09 · SENSITIVITY

Where the unit breaks

LTV:CAC across monthly churn and CAC, using the payment and margin currently set in the simulator above. Cells are labelled and shaded: ≥ 3x healthy, 1–3x marginal, < 1x loses money on every customer. The outlined cell is closest to the simulator's current inputs.

Two patterns are worth reading off a grid like this. First, the contours run diagonally: halving churn has the same effect on LTV:CAC as halving CAC, so a retention team and a growth team are working on the same number. Second, the grid is far more sensitive at low churn. Moving from 2% to 1% doubles LTV, while moving from 8% to 7% barely registers. This is why retention improvements compound and why churn assumptions deserve the most scrutiny in any model.

10 · PITFALLS

Ten mistakes that make unit economics look better than they are

  • LTV on revenue, not margin

    Inflates LTV by the inverse of the margin, often three to five times.

  • Uncapped lifetime

    1 ÷ churn at 1% implies 100 months. Few companies have the data to support that.

  • Blended CAC with organic customers

    Hides what paid growth costs. When organic share falls, CAC jumps.

  • Flat CAC at 10× spend

    Channels saturate. Model CAC as rising with volume, as section 06 does.

  • Churn from the whole base

    Old, loyal customers mask fast churn in new cohorts. Use cohort curves.

  • Leaving out variable costs

    Shipping, payment fees, returns, incentives and support all belong in contribution.

  • LTV:CAC without payback

    Case A's Green line is 3.2x and needs $3.8M. Ratio alone says nothing about cash.

  • Ignoring the cost of capital

    A dollar of margin in year four is worth less than a dollar of CAC today.

  • Hardcoded numbers in formulas

    A model that cannot be changed cannot be trusted. Every assumption gets its own labelled cell.

  • No sensitivity analysis

    A single-point forecast hides how close the business is to its breakpoints.

11 · CHECKLIST

Questions an investor will ask of the case

  • What is the unit, and how is revenue per unit calculated after discounts and refunds?
  • Which costs are in contribution margin, and which have been left out?
  • Is CAC fully loaded? What is it by channel, and what share of customers are organic?
  • What do retention curves look like by cohort, and are newer cohorts better or worse?
  • What horizon is LTV calculated over, and why?
  • How many months does it take to recover CAC in cash, not just in accounting terms?
  • What happens to CAC if acquisition spend doubles?
  • Which costs fall with scale, at what volumes, and what evidence supports those tiers?
  • How deep is the cash trough at the planned growth rate, and when is it reached?
  • Which single assumption, if wrong by 20%, does the most damage?

Methodology notes

Case A follows the structure of David Skok's Blue/Red/Green teaching example. Churned customers are rounded to whole customers each month with Excel's ROUND. The Blue and Red cash low points match the original example exactly. Green's churn is entered as exactly 1/60, which gives ($3,780,960); versions that round Green's churn to 1.67% give a slightly different figure. Case B's company, figures and tiers are illustrative and do not describe any real business. EBITDA is used as a cash proxy, so working capital, capex, tax and financing are excluded.
